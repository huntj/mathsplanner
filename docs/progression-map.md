# Maths Progression Map: EYFS to Year 13 (England)

Oct 14, 2026 · @J

## How to use this map

This map lists every mathematics learning statement a student in England meets from Nursery to Year 13, each with a unique ID and the IDs it depends on, so it can be loaded straight into a web app as a prerequisite graph.

Each stage opens with a short narrative written in the student's voice ("This year I…"), followed by a table of "I can…" statements. Statements are paraphrased from the statutory sources listed at the end; the wording is simplified for students but the mathematical content is unchanged.

**ID scheme.** `Stage-Strand-Number`, for example `Y4-MD-03` is the third multiplication and division statement in Year 4. Stage codes run N (Nursery), R (Reception), Y1 to Y9, GF (GCSE Foundation, shared with Higher), GH (GCSE Higher only), AL (A level Maths), FM (A level Further Maths) and CM (Core Maths).

**Prerequisites.** The Needs column lists the IDs that should be secure before a statement is taught. Only the nearest direct prerequisites are listed; earlier ones are implied through the chain. Statements with no Needs entry are entry points.

**Specification choices.** GCSE and A level content follows Pearson Edexcel: GCSE (9-1) Mathematics 1MA1, A level Mathematics 9MA0 and A level Further Mathematics 9FM0. Core Maths follows the board noted in its section. National curriculum year allocations are the DfE's for KS1 and KS2; KS3 is set out by the DfE as a single key stage, so the Year 7, 8 and 9 split here is a recommended teaching order, flagged as such.

## The big picture

Every student follows one statutory path to 16, with national checkpoints in Reception, Year 4, Year 6 and Year 11, then continues maths to 18 on one of three routes.

The strands thicken as students move up: primary is dominated by number and calculation, ratio and algebra arrive formally in Year 6, and by GCSE Higher algebra is about a third of the marks. Students who leave Year 11 below grade 4 must keep studying maths post-16, usually towards a GCSE resit, so the GCSE statements double as the resit curriculum.

## EYFS: Nursery and Reception (ages 3 to 5)

By the end of Reception a child should meet the two statutory mathematics Early Learning Goals, Number and Numerical Patterns; shape, space and measure are taught but not assessed as goals. Nursery statements follow the non-statutory Development Matters (3 and 4 year olds); Reception statements marked ELG are statutory end-of-year expectations.

**My journey (Nursery).** I sing counting songs and chant numbers. I learn that when I count, the last number I say tells me how many there are. I can see small groups of 1, 2 or 3 without counting. I build with blocks, notice shapes and patterns around me, and talk about big, small, long and short.

**My journey (Reception).** I really get to know the numbers to 10: what they look like, how they split into parts, and which is more. I can see up to 5 instantly. I learn number bonds to 5 by heart and start learning bonds to 10 and doubles. I count past 20 and notice the pattern of tens. I make and continue patterns, and compare length, weight and capacity.

## Key Stage 1: Years 1 and 2 (ages 5 to 7)

KS1 secures counting, place value to 100, the four operations with concrete objects and pictures, and the 2, 5 and 10 times tables; the year-by-year statements below are the statutory national curriculum. End-of-KS1 tests became optional from 2023/24, so the main checkpoint is teacher assessment.

**My journey (Year 1).** I count forwards and backwards to 100 and read and write numbers to 20 in words. I learn addition and subtraction facts within 20, and solve one-step problems with objects and pictures. I start to multiply and divide by grouping and sharing, and I find halves and quarters of shapes and amounts. I measure and compare lengths, weights and capacities, recognise coins, tell the time to the half hour and name common 2D and 3D shapes.

**My journey (Year 2).** I understand tens and ones to 100 and use a number line. I add and subtract 2-digit numbers and see that addition can be done in any order but subtraction can't. I learn my 2, 5 and 10 times tables and use × and ÷ signs. I find ⅓, ¼, ⅔ and ¾, use standard units like cm, kg and °C, solve money problems, tell the time to 5 minutes, describe shapes by their faces and edges, make tally charts and read pictograms and bar charts.

## Lower Key Stage 2: Years 3 and 4 (ages 7 to 9)

Lower KS2 builds formal written methods, all times tables to 12 × 12, fractions and decimals to hundredths, and measuring with standard units. The statutory Multiplication Tables Check (MTC) takes place in June of Year 4: 25 on-screen questions, 6 seconds each.

**My journey (Year 3).** I work with numbers to 1000 and use columnar addition and subtraction. I learn my 3, 4 and 8 times tables and multiply a 2-digit number by a 1-digit number. I count in tenths, add fractions with the same denominator and find equivalent fractions. I measure perimeter, tell the time on 12- and 24-hour clocks, recognise right angles and parallel lines, and use bar charts and tables.

**My journey (Year 4).** I work with numbers beyond 1000, negative numbers and Roman numerals. By the end of the year I know all my times tables to 12 × 12 by heart. I use short multiplication, work with decimals to two places, round to the nearest whole number, find area by counting squares, convert between units, classify triangles and quadrilaterals, plot coordinates in the first quadrant and use line graphs.

## Upper Key Stage 2: Years 5 and 6 (ages 9 to 11)

Upper KS2 completes the primary curriculum: long multiplication and division, all four operations with fractions, percentages, ratio and the first algebra. The statutory KS2 tests in May of Year 6 are an arithmetic paper (30 minutes) and two reasoning papers (40 minutes each).

**My journey (Year 5).** I work with numbers to a million and decimals to three places. I know about factors, multiples, primes, squares and cubes. I multiply 4-digit numbers by 2-digit numbers and use short division. I compare and add fractions with related denominators, multiply fractions by whole numbers, and meet percentages. I convert metric units, find area and volume, measure angles with a protractor, reflect and translate shapes, and read timetables.

**My journey (Year 6).** I work with numbers to ten million, use long division and the order of operations. I add, subtract, multiply and divide fractions, and move freely between fractions, decimals and percentages. I solve ratio and scale problems, write simple formulae and solve equations with two unknowns. I find areas of triangles and parallelograms, angles in polygons, plot in all four quadrants, draw pie charts and calculate the mean. Then I sit my SATs.

## Key Stage 3: Years 7 to 9 (ages 11 to 14)

KS3 consolidates primary arithmetic and moves students from number into algebra, proportional reasoning, geometric proof and probability. The DfE sets KS3 as one block, so the Year 7, 8 and 9 split below is a recommended order, not statutory; it front-loads the core concepts in the DfE's non-statutory KS3 guidance (place value, proportional reasoning, algebraic notation) so Year 9 can start GCSE content.

**My journey (Year 7).** I make sure my arithmetic is solid, including with negatives, decimals and fractions. I learn to use letters for unknowns, simplify expressions and solve simple equations. I work with factors, primes and powers, use ratio notation, and find angles, areas and perimeters using formulae.

**My journey (Year 8).** I work with standard form, percentages and direct proportion. I expand brackets, rearrange formulae, solve linear equations and draw straight-line graphs. I learn about angles in parallel lines and polygons, circles, transformations, and I calculate probabilities and compare data sets.

**My journey (Year 9).** I meet Pythagoras' theorem, trigonometry in right-angled triangles, similarity and congruence, and inverse proportion. I solve simultaneous equations and inequalities, find the nth term, factorise quadratics and use Venn diagrams and sample spaces. By the end of the year I am starting GCSE work.

## Key Stage 4: Pearson Edexcel GCSE (9-1) Mathematics 1MA1, Years 10 and 11 (ages 14 to 16)

GCSE Mathematics is three 1 hour 30 minute papers of 80 marks each, sat at one tier: Foundation (grades 1 to 5) or Higher (grades 4 to 9, with a 3 allowed). Paper 1 is non-calculator; Papers 2 and 3 allow a calculator. Students sitting in 2025, 2026 and 2027 (including November resits) get an Exam Aid formulae sheet, and Pearson now gives the volume of a pyramid in the question ([Pearson update](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/news-policy/subject-updates/mathematics/update-on-ofqual-decision-on-formulae-sheets.html); [Pearson formulae notice](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/news-policy/subject-updates/mathematics/important-information-on-the-provision-of-formulae-in-gcse-mathematics.html)).

**My journey (Foundation).** I make my number skills secure and fast without a calculator. I get confident with percentages, ratio and proportion, because they are a quarter of my marks. I solve linear equations and simple quadratics, plot graphs, use Pythagoras and basic trigonometry, and work with area, volume, angles, probability trees and statistics. I aim for a grade 4 (a standard pass) or 5 (a strong pass).

**My journey (Higher).** On top of all the Foundation content I work with surds, fractional indices and bounds. A third of my marks are algebra: quadratics by completing the square and formula, algebraic fractions, functions, iteration, proof and graph transformations. I learn circle theorems, the sine and cosine rules, 3D trigonometry, vector proof, conditional probability, histograms and cumulative frequency. Grades 7 to 9 depend on solving unfamiliar multi-step problems.

**Reading the table.** IDs use the Pearson / DfE content reference (N1 to N16, A1 to A25, R1 to R16, G1 to G25, P1 to P9, S1 to S6). GF statements are tested on both tiers; GH statements are Higher only. Where a reference mixes both, it is split into a GF and a GH row.

## Key Stage 5: Pearson Edexcel A level Mathematics 9MA0, Years 12 and 13 (ages 16 to 18)

A level Mathematics is three 2-hour papers of 100 marks each, sat at the end of Year 13: Paper 1 Pure Mathematics 1, Paper 2 Pure Mathematics 2, and Paper 3 Statistics (Section A) and Mechanics (Section B). Pure is two-thirds of the qualification, a calculator is allowed throughout, and a formulae booklet is provided. Statistics questions draw on Pearson's large data set of UK and overseas weather data; most sixth forms ask for GCSE grade 6 or 7 to start.

**My journey (Year 12).** I take my GCSE algebra much further: proof, polynomials, the factor theorem, circles and the binomial expansion. I meet calculus for the first time and use it to find gradients, stationary points and areas. I learn exponentials and logarithms. In statistics I study sampling, the binomial distribution and my first hypothesis tests. In mechanics I model motion with constant acceleration and apply Newton's laws.

**My journey (Year 13).** I learn functions, partial fractions, radians and the full set of trig identities. I differentiate and integrate almost anything using the chain, product and quotient rules, substitution and parts, and I solve differential equations. I use numerical methods like Newton-Raphson. In statistics I use the normal distribution and conditional probability; in mechanics I work with projectiles, friction, inclined planes and moments.

**Reading the table.** IDs follow the specification numbering: AL-P for Pure topics 1 to 10, AL-S for Statistics topics 1 to 5 and AL-M for Mechanics topics 6 to 9. The Yr column is a recommended split: 12 covers the AS-level content, 13 the remainder.

## Level 3 Core Maths: Pearson Edexcel Mathematics in Context 7MC0

Core Maths is an AS-sized Level 3 qualification (180 guided learning hours, graded A to E) for students with at least a GCSE grade 4 who are not taking AS or A level Mathematics. Pearson's version is the Level 3 Certificate in Mathematics in Context (7MC0), one of four that attract DfE core maths premium funding in 2026/27 alongside AQA Mathematical Studies and OCR Core Maths A and B (MEI) ([DfE core maths premium](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/16-to-19-funding-core-maths-premium/16-to-19-funding-core-maths-premium); [AMSP](https://amsp.org.uk/universities/core-maths)).

Both papers are 1 hour 40 minutes with a calculator. Paper 1 Comprehension (60 marks, 40%) is based on a pre-released source booklet, now issued around 3 March each year. Paper 2 Applications (80 marks, 60%) has one task linked to that booklet and three new themed tasks. About 80% of the content is GCSE level and 20% goes beyond it; spreadsheet skills are assessed indirectly ([7MC0 specification](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/core-maths/Specification_L3_Certificate_in_Mathematics_in_Context.pdf); [Pearson March 2025 update](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/news-policy/subject-updates/mathematics/march-2025-maths-and-statistics-update.html)).

**My journey (Core Maths).** I keep maths going after GCSE because it helps my other subjects, like psychology, biology, geography or business. I study a real-world source booklet before the exam and learn to read, question and analyse real data. I use spreadsheets, regression and correlation, work out risk and probability, plan the best outcome with linear programming, and model money and growth with compound interest and series.

## Curriculum change watch

Everything above is the curriculum in force in October 2026, but England's maths curriculum is being rewritten: a new national curriculum will be taught from September 2028 and new GCSEs from September 2029, so the app's data model should expect statements to move between years.

For maths, the review recommends keeping the amount and type of KS1 to KS3 content but resequencing it for deeper mastery and less repetition, and redesigning KS2 tests with more mental arithmetic and reasoning ([Tes summary](https://www.tes.com/magazine/analysis/general/curriculum-review-recommendations-by-subject)).
